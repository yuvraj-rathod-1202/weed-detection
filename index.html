Question

Why it matters. A laser weeder destroys weeds without herbicide by heating the plant’s growing point. It needs a single aim point per weed — a bounding box is not enough, because the centre of a box around an irregular weed can fall on bare soil or on the crop next to it. Every shot that lands on a crop damages the harvest, so avoiding crops matters more than hitting every last weed.

What is predicted, and when. The prediction is made on the machine, frame by frame, as the camera passes over the field. At that moment the only information is the RGB image itself — no species list, no stem positions, no earlier frames.

Unit (one row) one plant instance (a weed or a crop) in one image; for the laser metrics, one shot
Model output for every predicted weed: a pixel mask and one aim point (x, y)
Target a shot is correct when its point lands inside a real weed and outside every crop
Decision rule the laser fires only when the model is at least X sure a plant is a weed, and not within 20 px of a plant it thinks is a crop
Main metrics share of weeds hit; share of shots that land on a crop; share of shots wasted on soil

Limits of the claim. All evidence comes from offline photos: one main dataset recorded in European fields with one camera, evaluated image by image. Nothing here is a field trial, says how much laser energy kills a plant, or shows that the model works on other farms, crops or cameras. A small gallery of photos from a different farm (Results) shows how quickly that can break.

Data

Main dataset: CropAndWeed (Steininger et al. 2023). top-down RGB photos (1920 × 1088 px, about 0.4 mm per pixel) of crop rows with weeds, recorded in recording sessions. It is one of the few public datasets that label both crops and weeds, which this question needs: a weeds-only dataset cannot tell us whether the laser would hit a crop.

  • Annotations: a box and stem position for every plant, plus a pixel mask that gives each pixel its species. We use the authors’ own two-class variant CropOrWeed2: Crop (maize, sugar beet, soybean, sunflower, potato, pea, bean, pumpkin) and Weed (57 weed species).
  • Two recording sets: ave = the Application set (real farm fields) and vwg = the Experimental set (test plots). Real fields are the harder, more realistic case.
  • Access limits: non-commercial use only, and no redistribution of images or annotations. The data is downloaded from the official source in each run.

Quality issues that shape the method.

  1. Plants outside the two classes. annotated plants ( of all) are species outside CropOrWeed2 or are labelled only “Vegetation” because the annotators could not identify them. They are kept as ignore regions: a prediction on them counts neither as right nor wrong.
  2. Masks are per class, not per plant. Two touching weeds share one blob of “weed” pixels, but the laser needs one point per plant. Preprocessing explains how the plants are separated.
  3. Many plants are tiny. Weeds outnumber crops about to 1, and of weeds are at most 24 px across (about 1 cm) — a few pixels once the image is shrunk for the network.
  4. Near-duplicate frames. Consecutive photos in a session overlap heavily, so a random split would put almost the same image in training and test.
  5. Imbalanced crops. A few crop species dominate (chart below); rare ones are harder to learn.

Earlier dataset: CottonWeedDet12 (Lu et al. 2023; Dang et al. 2023). Photos from US cotton fields with boxes for 12 weed species (5,648 in the full release; a 2,391-image subset — 1,554 / 478 / 359 for train / validation / test — was used). The first experiments used it, but it labels no crops, so it can show weed finding but not crop safety. That gap is why the project moved to CropAndWeed; five of its photos still appear in the gallery.

Preprocessing

All steps are deterministic rules or come from the dataset itself; no statistic is fitted on the data, so nothing can leak from validation or test into training.

  1. Labels → two classes. Each species id is mapped to Crop or Weed with the official CropOrWeed2 table; unmapped species and “Vegetation” become ignore regions.
  2. Per-plant masks. For each plant box, the mask is the class’s pixels inside that box. Where that is ambiguous — two overlapping boxes of the same class, or a box with no class pixels — the image is passed to SAM 2.1 (Ravi et al. 2024) with the box as a prompt, and SAM’s mask is cut back to the class’s pixels, so SAM can only split plants, never invent leaves. Each mask is stored as its largest outline (polygon).
  3. Split by recording session, before any model is trained. Whole sessions go to train / validation / test (65 / 20 / 15 % of images, seed 42), so near-duplicate frames never cross splits. The split is fixed in configs/datasets/cropandweed.yaml and rebuilt identically on every machine.
  4. Model input. Images are resized to the model’s input size (1024 or 1280 px wide, aspect kept) and scaled to 0–1 by dividing by 255.
  5. Augmentation (training only). Random mosaics, scale and colour jitter and horizontal flips; experiment 5 adds vertical flips and ±15° rotations, since a top-down photo is equally valid upside down.

Who sees which split. Training uses train only. Validation picks the best training epoch, the model and the laser’s confidence threshold. Test gives the reported numbers (Evaluation).

Baseline and methods

Baseline: “every green blob is a weed”

Without machine learning, a weeder could aim at anything green. The baseline computes the vegetation index ExG − ExR (Meyer and Neto 2008) on brightness-normalised colours and keeps pixels where it is positive (its published threshold, no tuning), cleans specks with a 3 × 3 opening, and treats every connected blob of at least 64 px (8 × 8, smaller than the smallest labelled plants) as a weed, aimed at with the same point method as the model. It cannot tell crops from weeds, so it has no crop safety gate. If a trained model is worth having, it must beat this on crop safety without losing too many weeds.

The textbook ExG index with an Otsu threshold (Woebbecke et al. 1995) was tried first, on two validation images only: the greenish tint of the soil in these photos turned it into hundreds of soil fragments per image, so the published ExG − ExR rule replaced it.

Model: YOLO11s-seg instance segmentation

YOLO11s-seg (Jocher and Qiu 2024) is a one-stage instance-segmentation network (10 M parameters). For every detected plant it predicts a box, a class score (Crop / Weed) and 32 mask coefficients that are combined with shared prototype masks into a pixel mask. It starts from weights pretrained on everyday photos (COCO) and is fine-tuned on CropAndWeed.

  • Objective: a sum of box-regression loss, class loss (binary cross-entropy) and per-pixel mask loss, minimised with AdamW for up to 80 epochs; training stops early when validation accuracy has not improved for 20 epochs, and the best validation epoch is kept.
  • Why this method: the laser needs one point per plant, so plain semantic segmentation (one class per pixel) is not enough, and a box alone puts the aim point off the plant. A one-stage model runs in real time on a modest GPU, unlike two-stage models such as Mask R-CNN (He et al. 2017).
  • Assumptions: a top-down view at a fixed height, plants visible (not hidden under leaves), labels that are right on average, and test fields that look like training fields. The gallery tests the last one.
  • Three trained candidates: exp 3 at 1024 px input, exp 4 at 1280 px (small weeds get more pixels), exp 5 at 1280 px with vertical flips and rotations. A fourth run at full 1920 px did not finish in time.

From mask to laser point

The aim point is the mask pixel that maximises (distance to the mask’s edge) × (a Gaussian around the mask’s centre, width = half the plant’s radius): it always lies inside the plant, near its centre, and not on a thin leaf tip. Simpler rules fail in predictable ways — the centre of mass can fall between the leaves of a star-shaped weed, and the box centre can fall on soil.

Crop safety gate

A shot is vetoed when its point lies within 20 px (about 8 mm) of a plant the model predicts as a crop — roughly the laser spot radius plus aiming error. The gate can only protect crops the model actually recognises; the Errors section shows what that means in practice.

Evaluation

Split and protocol

The split is fixed before any model is trained: whole recording sessions go to train (65 %), validation (20 %) or test (15 %), so near-duplicate frames never appear on both sides. Then:

  1. Train on train only. Ultralytics keeps the epoch with the best validation mask accuracy.
  2. Choose on validation. Every candidate (exp 3, 4, 5) is run once on the 1,609 validation images, and every predicted weed down to confidence 0.05 is saved with where its aim point lands. Any confidence threshold can then be evaluated offline, without re-running the model.
  3. The rule, fixed before looking at the validation numbers: for each candidate, the threshold with the most weeds hit while at most 2 % of shots land on crops; the final model is the candidate whose best threshold hits the most weeds (ties: the faster model).
  4. Test, for the final model at its chosen threshold and for the baseline.

Metrics

Metric Definition Why it matters for a laser weeder
Weeds hit share of real weeds with at least one fired shot inside their mask weeds left alive keep competing with the crop
Shots on crops share of fired shots that land inside a crop plant each one damages the harvest — the costly error
Shots on soil share of fired shots that land on neither a weed nor a crop wasted laser time; harmless to the crop
Shot precision share of fired shots that land inside a weed 1 − crops − soil
Mask mAP50-95 standard instance-segmentation accuracy on validation secondary: how good the masks are, independent of the laser
Inference time ms per 1920 × 1088 image, one Tesla T4 GPU the machine must keep up with its driving speed

Shots on plants that are marked “ignore” (unidentified plants) are left out of every count. Uncertainty: 95 % intervals come from resampling whole recording sessions (2,000 bootstrap draws), because images of one session are not independent.

Reading the bars. Drag the slider down: the green bar grows (more weeds hit), and so does the grey one (more shots on bare soil). The red bar does something surprising: it shrinks. A lower threshold also lets more uncertain crop predictions reach the safety gate, which then vetoes more shots near crops. So the share of shots on crops is about 1 % at 0.05 and goes above 2 % only around 0.5–0.7. The 2 % rule therefore never stops the threshold from going down, and the optimum sits at 0.05, the lowest value evaluated; an even lower threshold might score higher still. What the rule does not price is waste: at 0.05 more than a quarter of shots hit bare soil, against about 16 % at 0.25. A real machine might accept fewer weeds hit for less wasted laser time; the slider shows that trade-off directly.

Validation loss stops improving around epoch 50 while training loss keeps falling, and validation mask accuracy plateaus; the kept weights are from epoch of . A development-time check (not reproduced in the repository; it used local test images) scored exp 5 the same way on 297 training and 297 test images: large weeds were found far more often on training images than on new ones (95 % vs 82 %: overfitting), while weeds under 24 px were missed even on training images (71 % vs 66 %) — pointing to a resolution limit rather than a lack of data. Limitations

  • The chosen threshold sits at the edge of the explored range (0.05), and the 2 % rule ignores wasted soil shots. A different but equally defensible rule (for example a cap on soil shots) would choose a higher threshold and hit fewer weeds.
  • One dataset, one camera. CropAndWeed comes from European fields with one camera setup; the Sugar Beets gallery shows a large drop on a new farm and camera. No claim is made for other crops, regions or machines.
  • One training run per configuration. Seeds were not repeated, so differences between experiments include training noise; the paired bootstrap covers sampling of fields, not of training runs.
  • Labels are imperfect. Tiny plants have rough outlines; 30 % of annotated plants are outside the two classes and ignored; per-plant masks for touching plants come partly from SAM.
  • Offline image metrics only. No field trial, no laser physics, no motion blur or timing. A shot “inside the mask” is assumed to kill the weed.
  • The 1920 px run did not finish (out of GPU memory, then out of time), so the resolution question is open.
  • Prediction, not causation. The analysis shows where the model fails (small plants, sunflower, one soybean field); it does not prove why — size, appearance and field conditions are entangled.

Conclusion

On held-out CropAndWeed test fields, an instance-segmentation model with a crop safety gate hits about 85 % of weeds while putting about 1 % of its shots on crops — far safer than aiming at every green blob, which hits fewer weeds and puts almost a quarter of its shots on crops. The price is waste: at the chosen threshold more than a quarter of shots land on bare soil, and the threshold choice depends on how one values wasted shots against missed weeds. The remaining failures are tiny weeds and crops the model does not recognise (sunflower, very young soybean), and the model does not transfer to a new farm and camera without new data. The next steps would be full-resolution input for small weeds, more varied training fields, and a selection rule that prices soil shots explicitly.
